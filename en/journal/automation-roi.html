What a repetitive task really costs you (and what automation returns)

By ORBE IA April 21, 2026 6 min read

Before automating anything, one question is worth asking: is it actually worth it? Automation has a cost — time to set up, sometimes a subscription to a tool. To decide with a clear head, you need to weigh that cost against what the task is already taking from you, every week, without you noticing.

The good news is that you can put numbers on all of this with a simple method, in a few minutes. No complicated spreadsheet, no finance background required. Just a bit of honesty about the time you really lose.

Step 1 — Measure the real time

The first mistake is to underestimate a task because it looks short. "It only takes me two minutes" is rarely true.

A repetitive task costs you on three fronts:

  • Doing it. The action itself: copying data, sending an email, updating a file.
  • Thinking about it. Remembering it needs doing, finding the right document, picking up the thread again. This mental load is invisible but real.
  • Fixing errors. When you do things by hand, you make mistakes. And correcting them often takes longer than the original task.

Once you have a per-occurrence estimate, multiply it by how often the task happens. That is where the numbers start to mean something.

Take a ten-minute task done every working day. Ten minutes is nothing. But across a year, it adds up to roughly 40 hours. A full working week, swallowed by a single small task.

Step 2 — Put a value on that time

Time only carries weight once it has a price. There are two ways to work it out, depending on your situation.

If you are a freelancer, use your billable rate. Every hour spent on an admin task is an hour you are not invoicing a client. The cost is a straight loss of earnings.

If you employ someone, think in terms of loaded cost: salary, taxes, tools, workspace. The true cost of an hour of work is well above the net wage on the payslip.

Apply that rate to your 40 annual hours, and the hidden cost suddenly becomes very concrete. A task that "only took ten minutes" turns into several thousand pounds a year.

Step 3 — Factor in the cost of errors

Time is not the only expense. Manual tasks generate mistakes, and some of them are costly.

  • A forgotten invoice is cash that never arrives.
  • A mis-copied figure in a quote or a return is a dispute or a penalty waiting to happen.
  • A client you forgot to chase at the right moment is a sale that goes to a competitor.

Individually, these incidents are rare. But their financial impact often dwarfs the time lost. A single forgotten invoice can be worth more than a whole year of the task itself.

You do not need an exact figure. Simply estimate how often an error occurs and how serious it is on average, then add it to the calculation. The total climbs quickly.

Step 4 — Compare it to the investment

You now have an annual cost: time valued, plus the cost of errors. What remains is to compare it to what the automation costs.

An automation has two parts:

  • Setting it up. A one-off cost: designing the flow, connecting it to your tools, testing it.
  • Running it. A recurring cost, usually low: a subscription to a service, sometimes nothing at all.

The comparison is revealing. When a task costs you several thousand pounds a year and the automation is a single modest outlay, the return on investment is measured in months, not years. Many automations pay for themselves before the first quarter is out — and then keep returning value without asking for anything more.

Beyond the numbers

Not everything fits in a spreadsheet. Some benefits matter as much as money.

Automating a task first means less mental load. You stop holding onto the things you must not forget. That freed-up space is felt straight away.

It also brings calm. No more race against the clock at month-end, no more chasing done in a panic. Work becomes steadier and less reactive.

And finally, it buys time to grow. The hours you recover do not vanish: they go towards what moves the business forward — finding clients, creating, looking after relationships. The part that cannot be automated.

The right question

We often frame the problem the wrong way round: "How much will this automation cost me?"

The better question is the opposite:

How much does it cost me not to have it?

As long as the task stays manual, you are paying. Every week, every month. The bill simply never shows up anywhere. It hides in scattered minutes and the occasional error.

A worked example

Take a data-entry task — pulling information from a form and copying it into your management tool. Allow around twenty minutes per working day, including the checks.

Across a year, that comes to nearly 80 hours. Two full-time weeks spent copying and pasting.

Value those hours at your rate, add the cost of the few data-entry errors that are bound to slip through, and the total easily reaches several thousand pounds a year. An automation that carries that data across by itself is set up once, runs for a modest sum, and wipes out almost all of that cost.

The cost of the status quo

The temptation is to change nothing. "It has always worked this way." That is understandable, but misleading.

Doing nothing is not free. The cost does not disappear because you ignore it: it is simply spread out and invisible. A few minutes here, an error there, a follow-up forgotten now and then. Taken on their own, none of these is serious. Added up over a year, it becomes a major expense that nobody sees go past.

The status quo has a price. The only difference is that it appears on no invoice.

Decide with method

You now have everything you need to make the call, without guesswork or rushing.

  • Measure the real time a task takes: doing it, thinking about it, fixing errors.
  • Value that time at your rate, and add the cost of errors.
  • Compare that annual cost to the investment an automation requires.

And when several tasks are candidates, prioritise by the gain-to-effort ratio. Start with the ones that cost a lot and are simple to automate. You get a quick result, you build confidence, and you fund the next projects with the time you recover.

Automation is not a question of fashion or technology. It is an economic decision. With these few steps, you know exactly which one to make.
