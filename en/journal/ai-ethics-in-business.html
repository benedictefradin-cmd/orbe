AI in business: the ethical stakes you can't ignore

By ORBE IA May 3, 2026 6 min read

Adopting artificial intelligence is not only about saving time or cutting costs. It also means taking on a responsibility. The moment a tool makes or prepares a decision that affects a person — an applicant, a customer, a supplier — ethical questions appear. And contrary to a widespread assumption, these questions are not reserved for large groups or tech giants.

A small business that screens applications, grants or refuses a commercial advantage, or replies automatically to its customers is just as concerned. The stakes are identical; only the scale changes. Understanding these stakes before deploying a tool is how you avoid costly mistakes and build something that lasts.

AI reproduces what you feed it

An AI system invents nothing. It learns from data — examples from the past — and then reproduces what it has observed. If that data contains bias, the system reproduces it. Worse still, it can amplify it, because it applies the bias systematically, at scale, and without hesitation.

Take application screening. A tool trained on a company's hiring history learns to recognise the profiles already recruited in the past. If that company has, without intending to, mostly hired a certain type of person, the tool will favour that same type — and set the others aside. A human bias becomes an automated one, faster and harder to spot.

The problem is not that the machine is "malicious". It does exactly what it was taught. So vigilance is less about the algorithm and more about the data and the way you use it.

Knowing how a decision is made

When a tool influences a decision, you must be able to explain why. That is the principle of transparency. A person affected by a decision has the right to understand what is happening to them: why their application was rejected, why their case was classified in a particular way.

The danger here is the "black box": a system that produces a result without anyone being able to explain its logic. If you cannot say why your tool reached a given conclusion, you can neither correct it, nor defend it, nor answer for it. For a business, that is an untenable position.

Transparency does not mean publishing your code or mastering the underlying mathematics. It means being able to explain, in plain language, what a decision is based on and who can challenge it.

Accountability stays human

One essential point is often forgotten: AI bears no responsibility. The company using it is accountable for its results. If a tool produces an unfair or mistaken decision, you cannot hide behind "the algorithm did it". The legal and moral responsibility falls on whoever chose to deploy the tool.

The practical consequence is clear: sensitive decisions must keep human control. Anything that affects a person directly — a hire, a refusal, a penalty, an assessment — should never be left to the machine alone. Automation can speed up the preparatory work, but the final judgement remains a human matter.

Personal data

Deploying AI almost always means handling data, and often personal data. That means respecting the regulatory framework (in Europe, the GDPR). Three simple principles are worth keeping in mind:

  • Consent. People must know their data is being used, and for what purpose.
  • Limited use. You collect only what is necessary, and use it only for the stated goal — not for something else later on.
  • Security. Data must be protected, stored properly, and accessible only to those who genuinely need it.

These rules are not merely legal constraints. They protect your customers, and therefore your reputation. A leak or a misuse of data can destroy trust that took years to build.

Ethics as an advantage

Ethics is often presented as a brake. The opposite is true. For a small business, clear and responsible practices are a genuine point of difference. Customers are increasingly attentive to how their data is handled and how the decisions that concern them are made.

A company able to say "this is how we use AI, and this is what we don't let it do" inspires trust. In a market where many players adopt these tools blindly, rigour becomes an argument in your favour. Trust cannot be declared into existence: it is built through visible, consistent choices.

A concrete example of bias

Let us return to application screening, because it captures the whole problem. A company wants to save time and hands the first CV filter to a tool. The tool is trained on the last ten years of recruitment. If, over that period, the company mostly hired people from certain schools, certain backgrounds or certain profiles, the tool learns that a "good candidate" looks like those profiles.

The result: it mechanically reproduces the past. Candidates with a different background — who might have made excellent hires — are filtered out before a human ever sees them. The company believes it is gaining objectivity; in reality, it is automating and freezing its old habits in place.

The question of human control

The right approach is to define clearly what the machine does and what the human does. The machine prepares, proposes, flags; the human decides and validates.

In practice, a tool can rank cases by priority, spot inconsistencies, and draw attention to an unusual situation. But it is a person who reviews, who decides, and who takes ownership of the outcome. This division of roles is not a lack of confidence in the technology: it is the condition for using it without becoming its hostage.

Communicating about your practices

Once these principles are in place, say so. Being transparent about your practices reassures customers and partners alike. Explaining simply how you use AI, which decisions remain human, and how you protect data — that is what sets a serious company apart from one that follows the trend without thinking.

This communication needn't be technical. A few honest sentences on your website or in your conversations are enough to show that you have given the matter real thought.

Ethics isn't only a matter of principle

Beyond values, ethics is a matter of risk management. An undetected bias can lose you customers, damage your image, or even expose you to lawsuits. A poorly controlled automated decision can cost far more than the time it was meant to save.

Thinking about ethics from the start protects you as much as it protects others. It does not require a committee or a large budget: it requires asking the right questions before you deploy, and keeping a human at the centre of the decisions that matter. Building ethics in from the start always costs less than retrofitting it later.
