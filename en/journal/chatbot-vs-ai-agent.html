Chatbot or AI agent: which one for your business?

By ORBE IA May 6, 2026 5 min read

"We'd need a chatbot." This phrase comes up early in most conversations. And behind it there is almost always a need that has not yet been stated clearly. Sometimes a chatbot is more than enough. Sometimes it is not at all what is required — an agent is. And confusing the two leads straight to disappointment: a tool that answers questions nicely but does nothing concrete, or a project far too heavy for a need that amounted to displaying opening hours.

The two words circulate as if they meant the same thing. They do not do the same job. Taking a few minutes to understand the difference saves months of trial and error.

What a chatbot does

A chatbot is a conversation. Its domain is the exchange of messages. It answers questions, guides a visitor, points them to the right information, qualifies a request before it ever reaches you.

Its logic is simple: it waits to be prompted. Someone writes, it replies. Nobody writes, nothing happens. It makes an excellent receptionist — always available, never tired, able to handle a hundred requests at once. But its role ends where the conversation ends.

In practice, a good chatbot can:

  • answer frequent questions with no human involvement;
  • explain a service, a price, a procedure;
  • collect the relevant details before an appointment;
  • direct people to the right person or the right page.

What it does not do: act inside your tools, trigger a task, move a case forward on its own.

What an AI agent does

An agent, by contrast, pursues a goal and acts. It does not merely talk: it takes charge of a process from start to finish. You hand it a mission — "every booking must be recorded, confirmed and scheduled" — and it sees to it.

The essential difference: an agent works even when no one is talking to it. It watches, triggers, runs, checks. An order arrives? It processes it. A form is submitted? It carries the steps through. A deadline approaches? It follows up. It does not wait for a question to start working.

Where the chatbot lives inside the chat window, the agent lives inside your tools: your calendar, your inbox, your spreadsheet, your invoicing software. It connects what used to be siloed and keeps information flowing, without you having to copy anything across by hand.

How to choose

The distinction comes down to a single question: do you need to reply or to do?

  • If the heart of the need is to give information, to inform, to direct — a chatbot is enough.
  • If the heart of the need is to carry out tasks, to move a process along, to produce a concrete result — it is an agent.

The question seems obvious once it is asked. Yet it is the one most often skipped. People talk about the tool before they have named the work to be done.

Often, both together

Reality is rarely clear-cut. In many cases, the two work side by side.

The chatbot greets at the front: it answers the first questions, reassures, qualifies the request. Then it hands over to the agent, which processes everything behind the scenes, quietly. The visitor sees a smooth conversation; what they do not see is everything that fires off once their request is confirmed.

This is often the most effective combination: a visible layer of conversation, an invisible layer of action. But these really are two distinct building blocks, and you need to know which one carries the actual need.

Start from the need, never the tool

The right method does not begin with "chatbot" or "agent". It begins with a description: what do you want to happen?

Describe the scene as it should unfold. A customer asks for your opening hours: they get an immediate, accurate answer, at any time of day. A customer makes a booking: their appointment is recorded, confirmed by email, added to your calendar, and a reminder goes out the day before. Once the scene is clear, the solution follows on its own.

The order matters. The solution flows from the need, never the reverse. Choosing the tool first risks bending your need to fit a box that was never the right one.

An example for each case

Take a practice — medical, legal, it doesn't matter.

Chatbot case. The practice keeps fielding the same questions: opening hours, consultation fees, address, documents to bring. A chatbot on the website answers instantly, day and night, and frees the front desk from these repetitive calls. The need is to inform. A chatbot covers it entirely.

Agent case. The practice wants every booking to be automatically recorded, confirmed with the patient, added to the practitioner's calendar, with a reminder sent the day before. Here it is no longer about replying but about chaining actions across several tools. That is an agent's job.

Same practice, two different needs, two different tools. And often the two end up coexisting.

The cost of confusion

Getting the tool wrong is expensive, in both directions.

Putting a chatbot where an agent was needed leaves you with an assistant that answers prettily but does nothing: appointments still have to be entered by hand, cases chased, information copied across. The promise of automation stays a promise.

Putting a full agent where a simple chatbot would have done is the opposite mistake: a heavier, longer, costlier project than the real need. You brought out the artillery to display opening hours.

In both cases the problem is not the technology — it works fine. The problem is wrong sizing, and that almost always comes from a need that was poorly defined at the outset.

Evolving over time

Nothing forces you to build everything at once. The best approach is often to start simple, observe, then extend.

You can perfectly well begin with a chatbot that answers the common questions, watch what visitors actually ask, spot the repetitive tasks that keep coming back — then add an agent to automate the ones that warrant it. The tool grows with use, rather than anticipating needs that may never be confirmed.

This gradual path has a quiet advantage: every step rests on something real. You are not investing in a hypothesis, you are responding to an observation.

The decisive criterion

If there were only one thing to remember, it would be this question, to ask before any project: what do I want to happen without me?

If the answer is "my customers get the right information", you are looking for a chatbot. If it sounds more like "my appointments manage themselves, my follow-ups go out, my cases move forward", you are looking for an agent.

The rest — technology, platform, integrations — is just implementation. The real choice is made there, on that single sentence.
